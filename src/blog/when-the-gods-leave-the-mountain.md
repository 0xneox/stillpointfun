# When the Gods Leave the Mountain

Joshimath, the crown jewel of Chamoli district, is sinking. ISRO's satellite data showed the town sank 5.4 cm in just 12 days between December 27, 2022 and January 8, 2023. Over 800 buildings developed cracks. Families were evacuated, their condemned houses marked with red X.

And It's not even the deepest part.

## The Prophecy That Was Already Written

Inside the ancient Narasimha Temple in Joshimath, established by Adi Shankaracharya in the 8th century sits an idol of Lord Narasimha, lion-faced avatar of Vishnu. And for centuries, priests and pilgrims have noted something quietly terrifying about this idol.

The right hand of the Narasimha idol is gradually becoming thinner over time.

There is an ancient prophecy: when the arm finally breaks off, the route to Badrinath will close forever and the deity will move to Bhavishya Badri. The Sanat Kumar Samhita states that when the arm of Lord Narasimha in Joshimath detaches itself from the body of the Lord, the way to current Badrinath will get destroyed. The Nara and Narayana hills will collapse, blocking the path to Badrinath shrine.

Ancient text. Sinking modern town. Cracking temple. The same hill.

The right hand of the Narsingh idol is growing smaller day by day. When it finally gets detached, the shrine will shift to Bhavishya Badri, the Future Badri.

## The Temple That's Been Waiting

22 kilometers from Joshimath, at an altitude of 8,530 feet in Suvai village, sits the Bhavishya Badri Temple. The prophecy says that when the arm of the Narasingha idol at Joshimath falls off and the mountains of Jay and Vijay near Vishnuprayag collapse, making the present shrine of Badrinath inaccessible, the worship of Lord Vishnu as Badrinarayan will begin at Bhavishya Badri.

At Bhavishya Badri, there is another idol of Lord Badrinath which is said to have originated on its own and is growing bigger each day.

One idol shrinking—its arm thinning year by year—in a sinking town.

Another idol growing—self-manifesting, swelling out of solid rock—in a remote forest 22 km away.

The scriptures encoded this 1,200 years ago. The geology is now performing it in real time.

## The Ghost Villages

The crisis is wider. Between 2008 and 2018, 500,000 people left Uttarakhand. In just four years from 2018 to 2022, 335,000 more people abandoned the state. The state's own Rural Development and Migration Commission now counts 1,787 villages as fully abandoned.

Drive through Pauri Garhwal, Chamoli, or Tehri on any given afternoon and you will find them: houses padlocked from the outside, terraced fields swallowed by forest, temples with no one left to light the oil lamp.

This is the part nobody reports. It's not just people leaving, the deities are following them out.

There are more than two hundred folk gods and goddesses in the mountains. The folk deities of villages facing the exodus are waiting for devotees, locked in their respective temples. These temples are opened once a year at the time of worship. There is a day's brightness and then people return to their respective destinations. What happened is that people who did not return to their ruined villages established their deity in the cities. The folk deities of the people also fled.

These are not just statues. These are living, breathing community identities, Golu Devta, Nanda Devi, Jhali-Bali, Devi hyper-local deities worshipped in a specific valley, on a specific hill, tied to the specific ecology of that place. When they get relocated to a flat in Dehradun or a colony in Delhi, something irreplaceable is severed. The deity survives. The sacred geography does not.

## The Languages Are Dying With the Villages

Migration statistics record the bodies that leave. They cannot record what goes with them. Garhwali and Kumaoni, the state's two dominant regional languages, are both listed in UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger. A century ago, over 80% of Uttarakhand's population spoke them; today that figure sits at 40 to 50%, concentrated among increasingly elderly, increasingly remote communities.

Two lesser known dialects; Rangkas and Tolcha—spoken in Pithoragarh district have already gone completely extinct. The Bangani dialect of Uttarkashi has been placed in the "critically endangered" category.

## The Char Dham Paradox

Here is Devbhumi's darkest irony. The very religious identity that makes Uttarakhand sacred to a billion Hindus is being weaponized to destroy it.

The number of visitors to Badrinath increased from 1.8 lakh in 2014 to 14.35 lakh in 2024, an eightfold rise. For Kedarnath, the number rose from 40,800 to 16.52 lakh, a 40x increase. An estimated 6 million pilgrims are projected for 2025.

The state government calls this a blessing. Scientists call it an accelerant.

At a time when the Himalaya is heating up three times faster than the global average, the government in 2016 initiated the Char Dham Pariyojana—a blanket road-widening project across 900 km of fragile mountain terrain. The government hopes to see 6.5 crore visitors to Uttarakhand in 2025, while the state's own population is merely 1.26 crore.

Pilgrims are being fast-tracked into "spiritual tourists," requiring amenities and entertainment. The Char Dhams are becoming char daams—devotional pilgrimage has become exploitative tourism.

## Where Science and Scripture Meet

Uttarakhand may be 1.6–1.9 degrees Celsius warmer by 2050. Districts at higher elevations—including Uttarkashi, Chamoli, Rudraprayag, and Pithoragarh are warming fastest. Residents are already experiencing changing temperatures, upward-moving snowlines, receding glaciers, erratic rainfall, drying perennial streams, and shifting cultivation zones.

Uttarakhand is home to more than 1,200 glaciers, many retreating rapidly. Melting glaciers form unstable glacial lakes held by loose soil and rock moraines, prone to bursting. Scientists warn that expanding lakes near Gangotri, Pindari, and Milam are "ticking time bombs."

The prophecy said the Ganga would return to the heavens when humans polluted it. The science says the glacier that feeds the Ganga is dying. The mountain that houses Badrinath is collapsing. The arm of the Narasimha idol is thinning. The villages are emptying. The temples have no one to light the lamp.

## The Deepest Truth

The Puranas did not predict a punishment. They predicted a transition. Kali Yuga ends. A new Satya Yuga begins. The old sacred geography disappears; not because the divine abandons the world, but because the world has to be remade. The gods don't die. They move. Badrinath moves to Bhavishya Badri. The Ganga returns to the heavens to descend again, renewed.

The gods are already in the habit of leaving and returning. The Puranas say that one day, they simply won't come back to the old address.

What the folk deities of the ghost villages are doing right now—migrating from locked hilltop temples to urban apartments in Dehradun and Delhi—is a microcosm of what the great Puranic prophecy describes at the cosmic scale. When humans leave, the sacred leaves with them. The gods are not omnipotent landlords. They are companions. They go where their people go.

When there was no one left in the village, the folk deity also had to migrate and be displaced elsewhere.

This is Devbhumi's deepest paradox. The Land of Gods is emptying of gods, not because divinity departed, but because the people who carried the divinity in their daily rituals, their seasonal festivals, their agricultural cycles, their Van Panchayat forests, their Garhwali songs and Kumaoni prayers—they left. And the gods, faithful companions that they are, followed.

## Is This Preventable?

At a certain level, what is happening to Uttarakhand is not fully preventable. Not anymore.

The Himalayas are geologically the youngest mountain range on Earth; still rising, still settling, inherently unstable by nature. Joshimath was built on ancient landslide debris. That was always going to move again. The glaciers began retreating long before humans burned a single coal plant. The Ganga has been changing course for millions of years. Kali Yuga or no Kali Yuga, the mountain was always going to shift.

What humans did was not create the process. They accelerated it, compressed ten thousand years of natural change into fifty years of reckless construction, deforestation, blasting, dam building, and mass tourism. The dam didn't cause the avalanche. It just made sure workers were in the tunnel when the mountain decided to move.

So there are two rivers running simultaneously here, one that was always going to flow regardless of human action, and one that humans made flow faster than it should. You can dam one. You cannot dam both.

## The Quality of the Unfolding

The Puranas never frame Kali Yuga as a mistake. They frame it as a season. The universe breathes in and out. Satya, Treta, Dwapara, Kali—not a fall from grace but a full rotation. Like a human life,childhood cannot be preserved into old age. Old age cannot be reversed into childhood. Each stage has its own completeness, its own dignity, its own inevitable direction.

The Bhavishya Badri prophecy is not a warning. It is a promise. The divine doesn't abandon the earth. It relocates. It finds a new address. The idol growing in the forest in Subhain village self-manifesting, enlarging year by year, that is not an ending. That is a beginning, patient and unhurried, already preparing for the moment the old road closes.

The Ganga returning to the heavens in the Puranas is not death. It is rest. Like a river going underground—invisible, but still moving, still sacred, still returning.

The villages didn't have to empty this fast. The languages didn't have to die this generation. The arm of the Narasimha idol may have been destined to thin, but did it have to thin while a hydropower tunnel was being blasted 300 metres beneath the town?

The glaciers were always going to retreat—but did Gangotri need to receive 400 times more black carbon in summer than in winter from unmanaged forest fires?

There is a difference between a river finding its natural course over centuries and a river being redirected by a bulldozer in a decade.

So what flows naturally and what was pushed?

The migration natural; and pushed.
The geological subsidence natural; and pushed.
The cultural erosion natural; and pushed.
The glacier retreat natural; and catastrophically pushed.

The Puranas seem to account for this too. Kali Yuga is defined not by catastrophe itself, but by the quality of human choice within catastrophe. The defining sin of Kali Yuga is not disaster, it is indifference. The slow looking away. The committee report of 1976 that warned Joshimath would sink if people kept building and the building that continued anyway. The scientists who said the glaciers were ticking bombs and the tunnels dug underneath them regardless.

The Puranas saw that too. That is precisely what Kali Yuga is.

## What This Really Is

What you are sensing—this feeling that it is flowing, that it cannot be stopped, that is not hopelessness. That is something more ancient than hope or despair. It is the recognition that you are watching a living system complete a cycle that began before recorded history and will end after recorded history.

Devbhumi is not dying. It is changing form. The way fire changes wood—not destroying it, but releasing what was always stored inside it. The ash feeds the next forest. Bhavishya Badri is already there, waiting in the trees, its idol growing larger in the silence, patient as stone, ready to be the next address of the divine.

The ghost villages are not failures. They are seeds, locked houses holding the memory of a way of living that the mountain will ask for again, in its next breath, in its next age, when the conditions are right and the river returns from wherever rivers go when they go home.

Everything is flowing. Everything has always been flowing. The Himalayas themselves are flowing—just slowly enough that we mistake them for solid ground.

The real question is not whether this can be stopped.
The real question is: how do you flow with it without losing what matters?
How do you carry the folk deity with ceremony when you leave?
How do you teach your child the Garhwali word for the sound water makes in a mountain stream—even in a Dehradun flat, even in a Mumbai apartment, so that when the stream comes back, the word is still alive to receive it?

That is the only freedom left inside an inevitable current.
Not to stop the river.
But to remember what you are carrying as it takes you where it goes.
