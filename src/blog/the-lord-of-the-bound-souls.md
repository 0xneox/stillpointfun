# The Lord of the Bound Souls

What if the weapon that destroys you is also the thing that liberates you? What if the missile that burns through three cities is the same fire that burns through the rope around your neck?

The Pashupata tradition has an answer that's been disturbing me for months. They call it the Pashupatastra—Shiva's ultimate weapon, granted only to Arjuna and a handful of sages. But the deeper you go, the more you realize: this isn't a weapon at all.

The entire system rests on three Sanskrit words that contain the whole universe: Pati, Pashu, and Pasha.

Pati is Shiva as Pasupati, the absolute Lord. Not a deity among deities but the ground of all being. According to the Varaha Purana, Shiva is called Pashupati because he resides inside the body of all living beings as their protector.

Pashu is every soul, every creature, every human. The word literally means "animal" but its deeper meaning is "that which is bound." You are Pashu not because you're inferior—but because you're tethered. The word designates that which is dependent on something else.

Pasha is the noose. The rope of bondage. The chains.

The entire spiritual mission of the Pashupata tradition is one sentence: cut the Pasha to liberate the Pashu into union with Pati. The Pashupatastra—Shiva's cosmic weapon—is the instrument that severs that rope.

Before anything else—before the weapon, before the ritual, before even the philosophy—there is a wound.

Anava mala is what makes us believe that we are a limited individual consciousness and not Shiva. This is the most devastating teaching in all of Shaivism. The wound isn't something you acquired. It's not your fault. It has no origin in time. Anava is an innate, beginningless positive entity that contracts the soul's naturally infinite consciousness into something finite and atomic.

You didn't fall from grace. You were never aware you had it. The Pashu—every soul—emerges into existence already wearing its blindfold.

On top of this root wound, two more chains grow: Karma and Maya. Three ropes. One neck. This is the Pashu's situation from time without beginning.

The Puranas say that in the 25th Mahayuga, when Vishnu incarnates as Vasudeva on earth, Shiva takes the incarnation of Lakulisha and enters a certain dead body in the burial ground of Kayavarohana—a Siddha Kshetra, a power site.

You have to parse this event carefully. It's not decorative mythology. It's a precise technical transmission.

A Siddha Kshetra is a place where the veil between worlds is thin—a location already saturated with liberation. Kayavarohana literally means "descent into the body." Shiva—who is Pasupati, the Lord of all Pashus—chose to inhabit a dead body. The most bound object in existence. A corpse. The thing furthest from liberation. He entered the maximum state of Pashu-hood and walked back out of it as Lakulisha, holding a club.

This is the entire Pashupata teaching demonstrated in a single cosmic act. The Lord descended into the deepest bondage, the literal death of the body, to show that even death is not outside his domain. Even the Pashu at its most extreme—dead, cold, finished—can be animated by Pati. The Pasha cannot hold the Lord.

In the second stage of the Pashupata Vrata, the aspirant leaves the temple and engages in anti-social behavior in order to attract verbal and physical abuse. The texts state that the aspirant's sins pass over to his abusers, and their merits pass over to him.

The ascetic—after the first stage of temple worship and purification—deliberately goes out into the world and acts like a madman. Limping. Babbling. Laughing at nothing. Making strange sounds. The crowd abuses him. They spit insults. They mock him.

The Pasupata Sutra teaches that karma is transferred through reaction. When someone abuses you and you do not react—no anger, no shame, no defence—then your accumulated sin-karma flows to them like water down a slope, and their merit-karma flows to you.

The ascetic is using the world's contempt as a spiritual mechanism. He's offering his ego to the crowd as fuel, and what flows back is purity.

The Pasha that holds the Pashu most tightly is Anava—the ego, the sense of being a separate self who can be shamed or praised. By inviting maximum shame and not flinching, the ascetic dissolves the very mechanism of Anava. He's burning the root impurity by submitting it, undefended, to the flame of public contempt.

This isn't humiliation. It's sophisticated psychological alchemy.

Even Indra—king of heaven—had to perform the Pasupata Vrata. The weapon that cleans even a god's worst sin is the same weapon handed to Arjuna. The same fire.

The most radical and beautiful element: the practitioner's body itself becomes the yajna kunda (fire altar).

The Atharvasiras Upanishad describes the Pashupata vrata as consisting of besmearing one's own body with ashes while muttering the mantra: "Agni is ashes, Vayu is ashes, Sky is ashes, all this is ashes, the mind, these eyes are ashes."

The ashes (bhasman) are called linga—the sign and mark of a Pashupata ascetic. One should collect ashes, bake them, and smear the body in the morning, midday, and afternoon.

Why ash? Because ashes are compared to the materiality of creation itself—the declaration is that ash is fire, water, earth, space, the mind, the eyes, the senses, and everything. When you smear ash on your body you're saying: "I am already dissolved. I am already what remains after the fire has burned through everything. I am beyond birth and death."

Bhasma is what remains after the yajna fire has consumed everything. When you smear ash on your body, you're declaring: I have already been burned. I am the residue of complete self-offering. There is nothing left in me to burn. This is the state beyond karma—because karma requires a subject who acts and an object who receives the fruit. The bhasma-body has already dissolved that subject-object split in the fire.

The mantra muttered during the ash-smearing is: "The ash is fire, the ash is water, the ash is earth, everything is ash, the ether is ash, the mind, the eyes and other senses are ash."

All five elements—earth, water, fire, air, space—declared to be ash. All sense organs—declared to be ash. Mind itself—ash. What's left? Only the awareness that remains after everything perceptible has been surrendered. That awareness isn't yours. It's Shiva.

Liberation comes directly from the grace of Shiva, and the theory of Karma and rebirth isn't given any importance in the Pasupata system.

This is where Lakulisha shattered the entire framework of Indian religion as it existed before him.

Every system prior—Samkhya, Yoga, early Vedanta—taught that liberation comes through the accumulated purity of the practitioner's own effort. You meditate enough, your karma burns down, and you become free. Liberation is the fruit of self-effort.

Lakulisha said no. According to Lakulisha, following liberation, the soul doesn't merge with Shiva; it continues to exist separately but free from pain. Lord Shiva practices no power over the liberated souls.

And the most radical clause: God can create changes in the world and in the destinies of men according to His own pleasure. God doesn't necessarily depend upon the person or his karma.

Shiva can liberate anyone. Anytime. Regardless of karma. Regardless of merit. This is pure grace—Shaktipata in its most absolute form—the descent of divine energy directly into the bound soul, cutting the Pasha not because the soul has earned it, but because Shiva chooses to.

The entire Pasupata Vrata—the ash, the chanting, the dancing, the abuse, the cremation ground, the years of practice—isn't earning liberation. It's making yourself available to be liberated. You cannot force the Pashupatastra to fire. You can only stand in its path and stop moving away.

Pashupatas disapprove of the Vaishnava theology, known for its doctrine of servitude of souls to the Supreme Being, on the grounds that dependence upon anything cannot be the means of cessation of pain. They recognize that those depending upon another and longing for independence will not be emancipated because they still depend on something other than themselves.

The Pashupatas attacked Vaishnavism—the devotional tradition of surrender to Vishnu—not out of sectarian rivalry but out of metaphysical precision. Their argument: if your liberation still depends on another—even if that other is God—then you're still bound. You've merely traded one dependency for a more comfortable one.

The Pashupata liberation is absolute. The Siddhesvara doesn't depend on Pasupati for their freedom. Pasupati is the efficient cause—the one who cuts the rope—but once the rope is cut, the soul stands entirely alone in its own infinite nature. Lord Shiva practices no power over the liberated souls.

This is why Lakulisha took a dead body: to demonstrate that even the most radical dependency—the body's dependency on life itself—can be transcended. A dead body has no agency. Shiva entered it. By entering the most helpless object in existence and walking it out of the cremation ground as a living teacher, Lakulisha demonstrated the teaching in its own form: the Pashupatastra of grace operates without the consent or cooperation of the Pashu. The rope can be cut from outside.

Lakulisha's Pashupata structure retained the basic idea of five categories but proposed that the ultimate aim of the soul is to attain divine perfection. He kept Lord Shiva as the sole material cause of the universe, thereby directing the Pashupata philosophy from basic dualism to dual-nondual. According to him, the soul—Pashu—is hindered from attachment to Lord Shiva by fetters or Pasha. The soul is able to retain its individuality even in its liberated form called Sayujya—defined as close to, but not union with, the Lord.

Sayujya is the terminal concept. Not merger. Not annihilation. Not Advaita's ocean dissolving the drop. The Pashupata liberation is closeness without merger—the soul retaining its distinct identity while existing in the fullest possible proximity to Shiva. You remain you. But you are you without the Pasha, without the Anava, without the false contraction. You are you at maximum—at the full bandwidth of what a soul can be—and in that full bandwidth you find that Shiva is not separate from you, because there is no longer any obstruction between you and Shiva, though there is still a distinction.

Lakulisha's dead-body-entry is the perfect image of Sayujya: the body (Pashu) remains a body, fully itself, still made of skin and bone—but animated entirely by Shiva's life-force. Not replaced. Not dissolved. Taken. Used. Loved into existence.

The club he carries—the Lakuta—is the weapon that breaks the Pasha. The same weapon as the Pashupatastra, held in a human hand, walking the dust roads of Gujarat, looking for the next student.

The Pashupata Astra Yajna isn't a ritual performed by a human being directed toward a distant God.

It's Shiva performing a ritual upon himself.

The Pashu is Shiva wearing his own amnesia as a costume. The Pasha is the rope Shiva tied around himself to play the game of forgetting. The Pati is Shiva remembering. The Pashupatastra is the moment Shiva fires the weapon of his own recognition at his own contraction, and the contraction dissolves.

The yajna fire is Shiva. The ash is Shiva. The ascetic smearing ash on his body is Shiva. The moment of liberation is Shiva releasing Shiva from Shiva's own bondage.

The Pashupatastra was never a weapon. It was always a mirror.

And when it fires—when that recognition strikes—what burns away isn't you. What burns away was never you. What remains has always been there.

The bhasma you wear after liberation isn't the ash of what you were. It's the ash of what you were not.
